\begin{abstract}
We present a reinforcement-learning framework for training small language models
to reason effectively on verifiable problems. The system performs approximate
$Q$-learning over an LLM-induced context Markov decision process: states are
structured contexts, actions are a small set of coarse cognitive verbs whose
textual realization is generated by the language model, transitions are sampled
from the model's response distribution, and the reward signal is anchored by a
non-LLM verifier such as a symbolic solver, a unit-test harness, or a proof
checker. The agent learns a parametric action-value function $\Qpsi(s, a)$ via
a multi-head $Q$-network that shares the hidden state of a frozen base model
through a small adapter. Bellman targets are formed from sampled transitions
$(s, a, r, s')$ and a slowly-updated target copy of the value head, with all
external reward originating from the verifier rather than from learned
aesthetic critics. Monte Carlo Tree Search enters as a policy improvement
operator: rather than serving as the core learning algorithm, it generates
locally improved estimates $\Qmcts(s, a)$ and visit-count policies $\pimcts$
that produce stronger Bellman targets during training, and it acts as a
test-time-compute knob at inference (a search mode for accuracy and a fast
greedy mode for latency). Expert iteration is the joint distillation of these
improved estimates into the parametric $Q$ and policy heads. We separate
verifier-anchored Bellman targets from learned-aesthetic signals that may only
serve as priors, reducing the Goodhart attack surface against the trained
agent. The paper presents the framework, the architecture, and a pre-registered
experimental plan with falsifiable hypotheses (a fixed baseline grid, a
FLOP-accounted compute axis, and a Holm-Bonferroni-corrected falsification
criterion committed before any training), and reports a toy-problem pilot
study that exercises the full pipeline end-to-end. The pilot shows that
a single round of expert iteration improves zero-shot held-out argmax agreement
by $15$ percentage points ($57\% \to 72\%$, single training seed) and reduces
mean $L_1$ to the MCTS targets by $42\%$, while a second round with
naively-expanded training data produces a structurally informative regression;
a controlled rebalancing experiment rules out class dilution as the dominant
regression mechanism, isolating distillation-target instability and
shortcut-pattern dependence instead. Beyond the offline pilot, we implement the
full online expert-iteration loop on an in-process $1.5$B substrate, with
prioritized replay, the combined $Q$-and-policy objective, and a
periodically-updated target network; a first closed-loop run provides the
honest per-decision compute accounting, and a fully instrumented run that
captures the across-iteration $Q$-loss trend and the prior-sharpening signal is
currently executing, with those learning-signal results pending. The pilot is
feasibility evidence for the framework; the large-scale
empirical claim against the pre-registered benchmark grid is deferred to a
follow-up study.
\end{abstract}
